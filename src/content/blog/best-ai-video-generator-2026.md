## Kling 4.0: The Length Champion

Kling came out of nowhere. A Chinese AI lab releasing a video generator that rivals Western competitors wasn't on my radar—until I saw the results.

### What Makes Kling Impressive

**30-second video generation.** This is Kling's killer feature. While Runway caps at 10 seconds and Sora struggles with consistency over 20 seconds, Kling reliably produces 30-second clips. For practical video production, length matters.

**Value for money.** The credit pricing works out cheaper per second than competitors. For volume generation, Kling's economics are compelling.

**Rapid improvement.** Version 4.0 jumped significantly from 3.0. The pace of improvement suggests Kling will keep closing quality gaps.

**Good motion coherence.** Objects and people maintain consistency across longer clips. Less of the "morphing" artifacts that plague competitors.

### Where Kling Falls Short

**Face generation is inconsistent.** People's faces can distort or change subtly through longer clips. Usable for distant shots, risky for close-ups.

**The interface is clunky.** Clearly designed for a Chinese audience first, with translation feeling like an afterthought. Navigating settings takes time.

**Prompt interpretation varies.** English prompts don't always translate to expected outputs. You'll iterate more to get what you want.

**Geographic concerns.** Some users worry about a Chinese platform for commercial content. The ToS is standard, but corporate policies may prohibit use.

## Runway Gen-3: The Reliable Workhorse

Runway has been in the AI video game longest. Gen-3 represents years of refinement, and it shows in the consistency and control you get.

### What Makes Runway the Industry Standard

**Predictable results.** I know what I'm going to get with Runway. The same prompt produces similar outputs. For professional work where predictability matters, this is undervalued.

**Camera controls are excellent.** Pan, tilt, zoom, dolly—Runway gives you cinematographic control that competitors don't match. Specify camera movements in prompts and they happen.

**4K output.** Runway is the only platform offering true 4K resolution. For production work heading to large screens, this matters.

**Ecosystem integration.** Runway's other tools (inpainting, motion brush, green screen) complement the video generator. It's a production platform, not just a generator.

### Where Runway Falls Short

**10-second limit is frustrating.** You'll stitch clips together constantly. For flowing scenes, this creates visible seams.

**Subscription model limits experimentation.** Fixed monthly credits mean careful rationing. I've hit limits mid-project and had to wait or upgrade.

**Physics can break.** Water, fire, and complex motion sometimes look wrong. Better than older models, but not at Sora's level.

**People still look slightly off.** Human subjects are improved but not perfect. Fine for B-roll, risky for hero shots.

## Sora: The Quality King

OpenAI's Sora launched with enormous hype and—unusually—largely delivered. For pure quality and complex scene understanding, Sora leads.

### What Makes Sora Special

**Physics understanding is remarkable.** Liquids flow correctly. Fabrics drape naturally. Objects interact plausibly. Sora understands the physical world better than competitors.

**Complex scenes work.** Multiple people, interacting objects, environmental effects—Sora handles complexity that breaks other generators.

**60-second potential.** While consistency degrades, Sora can generate up to 60 seconds. With the right prompt and luck, you get usable long-form content.

**Faces are the best available.** Human subjects look more natural, emote more believably, and maintain consistency better than alternatives.

### Where Sora Falls Short

**Generation time is slow.** 5-15 minutes per clip versus 1-2 for Runway. For iterative workflows, this drags.

**Unpredictable availability.** Capacity limits mean generation queues. During peak times, you wait. For deadline work, this is risky.

**Expensive at scale.** The credit system burns through faster than expected. Heavy users will feel the cost.

**Prompt sensitivity.** Small prompt changes create wildly different outputs. Finding the right prompt takes iteration.

## Can AI Video Replace Stock Footage?

I've now produced three client videos using primarily AI-generated footage. Here's my honest assessment:

### Where AI Video Works as Stock Replacement

- **Generic establishing shots** (cityscapes, nature, abstract)
- **B-roll that's not subject-critical** (backgrounds, transitions)
- **Conceptual/metaphorical footage** (technology concepts, business themes)
- **Content where "AI aesthetic" fits** (tech, futuristic, creative)

### Where Stock or Real Footage Still Wins

- **Recognizable locations** (AI can't do the real Eiffel Tower)
- **Specific people or actions** (hard to prompt precisely)
- **Legal/compliance content** (stock has model releases)
- **Ultra-high quality requirements** (4K stock is sharper)

My current ratio: About 60% AI-generated, 40% stock or original footage for typical projects.

## Text-to-Video vs Image-to-Video

All three platforms support both input types. Here's when to use each:

**Text-to-video works best for:**
- Abstract concepts
- Generic scenes
- When you want AI creativity
- Exploration/ideation phase

**Image-to-video works best for:**
- Specific visual styles
- Animating existing assets
- Maintaining brand consistency
- When you know exactly what you want

My workflow: Start with text-to-video for exploration, then use image-to-video with the best frames for final production.

## Practical Production Tips

After generating hundreds of AI videos:

**1. Prompt engineering matters.** Be specific about camera angle, lighting, movement. "Cinematic, slow motion, golden hour lighting, tracking shot" gets better results than "pretty video."

**2. Iterate quickly.** Generate 5-10 variations of important shots. The variation in outputs means some will be much better than others.

**3. Plan for post-production.** AI video rarely works straight from generator. Color grade, stabilize, add sound design. Treat AI output as raw footage, not final delivery.

**4. Composite strategically.** Use the best 3 seconds of a 10-second clip. Combine multiple generations. AI gives you options—use them.

**5. Know the limits.** Don't try to force AI to do what it can't. Hands, text, specific faces—work around these limitations rather than fighting them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use AI-generated video commercially?

Yes, all three platforms grant commercial rights on paid plans. You own the output and can use it in monetized content. Check individual terms for edge cases like political content or deepfakes.

### Will viewers notice AI-generated video?

Depends on the content and your audience. Generic B-roll passes easily. Close-ups of people trigger uncanny valley for some viewers. Context matters—tech content audiences expect and accept AI generation.

### How do I get consistent characters across clips?

This remains challenging. Image-to-video with consistent reference images helps. Sora handles character consistency best. For important characters, consider mixing AI backgrounds with real filmed subjects.

### What about copyright and training data?

Ongoing legal debates. Platforms indemnify users in their terms, but the landscape is evolving. For risk-averse commercial work, stock footage with clear licensing may still be preferable.

### Is AI video improving fast enough to wait?

Improvement is rapid—but tools today are already useful. Start learning now. Skills in prompting and AI-assisted production will transfer to better models. Waiting means falling behind.
